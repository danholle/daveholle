Hello, Dave
October 22, 1953

David Henry Holle was born this day.

His first name came from David Seever, Dave's great uncle, who was born Swiss in the late 1800s. Uncle David came to the USA early in life, and supported the US World War effort(s) as a fluent French speaker / interpreter.

Dad and Uncle David had legendary disagreements about politics. But their conversations were based on mutual respect. That respect moved Dad to name his first born son after Uncle David.

His middle name came from his maternal grandfather, Henry Martin Woaler, who came across the Atlantic from a small town east of Oslo, Norway. Henry settled first in Canada, then moved south to the (relatively) warm, southern environs of Illinois.

Grandpa Woaler passed away in a work accident in 1946, when Mom was only 18.

Dave was the first of 4 Holle boys to be born at Copley Hospital in Aurora. We were all named D Holle: Dave, Dan, Doug, Dean.

Our father (Donald) thought this would be a good idea: hand-me-downs could flow freely, with no need to change the name tag. This penchant for consistency took other forms: we were born in 1953, 1955, 1957, and 1959. Odd, isn't it?

Then, in 1964, Barbara came along. Obviously, she was different, at so many levels. They build Edwards Hospital in Naperville, just for her. In the process, she deftly evaded an avalanche of hand-me-downs.

I was surprised to see (in this announcement) that Mom and Dad lived in Aurora, Illinois when Dave was born. Perhaps they were only there temporarily, while Dad built our first family home in Naperville... on Webster, I think. From the ground up, I think. By himself, I think.

Later, Dad built another family home at 815 Douglas Avenue, in Naperville. All 7 of us called that place "home" for decades.

Late 1960s:
Family Christmas Traditions

Christmas 2025 approaches as I write this. Along with these pictures of Dave at Christmas, I'm reminded of the family traditions we had. By now there were 5 kids, ranging from Dave (the oldest) to Barb (the youngest).

The Christmas Tree would always sit in that corner of the living room, between the polished cherry hutch, containing all the really good, fragile, and (thus) never-used dishes; and the red sofa, which seemed miraculously indestructable (we tried).

Through December, us kids would put gifts under the tree. We would sometimes have evening pre-Christmas celebrations by the tree, with egg nog and fresh-cooked cookies (remember the anise cookies?). The pile of gifts was slowly growing.

On Christmas morning, we'd awaken to Christmas stockings hung from our bedroom doors. The living room door was sealed. We'd excitedly choke down breakfast, after which we'd line up, youngest to oldest, at the living room door.

Dad would ceremoniously open the door; and we'd rush in, to see the meager set of gifts that had been in evidence the night before had blossomed into a set of gifts that more or less filled the room.

Soon the room would be filled with spent wrapping paper, gifts, happy kids, and exhausted parents.

1969:
Dave's First Job
at WONC-FM

Dave was 16. That made him much older than his place of employment: WONC (89,1 FM) was started the year before, as the local radio station for North Central College, a small private college in Naperville.

To be fair, North Central College was older still. Founded in 1861, it was one of the oldest things in Naperville. Even older than our parents. WONC's offices were at the top of Old Main, one of the early buildings on campus, and the symbol of the school.

It was a pretty good choice as a first job. A first-time employee in a new organisation often adapt to each other. The employee grows into a role that fits them, and the organisation evolves to make the most of that nascent contribution.

That happened with Dave. He wrote local news stories. He fixed stuff. He broke stuff. Then he wrote more stories. And so forth.

Dave's first boss, Russ, kindly wrote this note on the back of a card to Dave:

Dear Dave, Science genius and Joe Reporter, you hang in there and keep writing those stories. I don't have to wish you good luck because you don't need it. We will have fun at the station this summer so stay alert! --- Your boss, Russ

I remember Dave using the codeword "Tungsten Fulminate" to refer to WONC. True nerds would catch the reference: WONC is the chemical formula for Tungsten (W) Fulminate (ONC). Not many people at WONC knew that. Now that I think about it, I'm pretty sure Dave was the only one.

Dave knew that because mercury fulminate, Hg(ONC)2, was used by Alfred Nobel to detonate dynamite, which had an explosive impact on Nobel's career. The rest is history.

I will hasten to add that Dave's association with Tungsten Fulminate did not have the same career impact, although he did have a blast at WONC.

1971-1973:
College of DuPage (COD)

Dave pursued a career with computers at COD. There, he met Darryl Van Nort, who worked in the COD data center.

This became, literally, a lifetime friendship, spanning over half a century. Their shared interests included not only computing, but also photography.

Footnote: Darryl passed away in 2023, from health issues involving COVID and heart disease. He was 72, plus a few weeks. Same age as Dave, by the way: Dave outlived Darryl by 7 days.

The classes in computing were pretty limited back then: the idea of a degree in "Computer Science" didn't exist yet. Dave would register for classes, and then the classes wouldn't run because not enough people signed up for them.

So Dave (possibly with Darryl's help) hacked the registration system, and enrolled fake students so his courses would run. These fake students had names like George Washington, Benjamin Franklin, Thomas Jefferson, etc. You know, your usual sorts of COD students.

Imagine the poor instructor, nervously addressing a nearly empty classroom on the first day, calling out names to see who was present...

October, 1983

Holle Family + Mutz - Dan

A family photo. Significant here, because it covers a time when we don't have many photos of Dave.

Mutz AKA Aunt Marge AKA Marge Sanderson was our next-door neighbour and adopted family member. She was a reasearch biologist at Argonne National Labs, one of many research labs in our area as we grew up. Their presence in general, and Marge in particular, added to our focus on science as a family.

Mutz lived on her own next door, but not really. She lent us her lawnmower, and we mowed her lawn. She shared produce from her prolific garden, especially fresh asparagus. She shared photos from her travels to Mexico, and her magazine subscriptions. We were the family she never had, and she was the Aunt Marge we never had.

1973:
Dave as Corporate Data Centre Wonk/h1>

Around 1973, Dave worked a corporate data processing job at CT Law Technology, which (as the name suggests) provided automation services for law firms.

By then, Darryl had a job at IBM; I was a senior in high school, with a part-time job at a Bell Systems subsidiary. All 3 of us worked with IBM mainframes:

  • Dave's company used an IBM 360/30 ($1.3M in today's money);
  • I used a 370/145 ($7M in today's money), and
  • Darryl had access to test machines that were much bigger and more expensive (so was Darryl). He used a 370/168, which cost around $20M.
Despite the colossal cost of these machines, they were painfully slow by contemporary standards. Your PC... your phone... even your watch... might have more compute power than these monster machines.

But they were the leading edge, at the time.

Dave's job at CT Law Technology was to write legal applications in IBM System/360 Assembly Language... also known as "machine language"... something Dave was good at.
We decided to have a drag race over the weekend, when these machines were usually idle. The test involved writing all prime numbers in sequence to a high-speed line printer. We'd see how long each machine could keep the printer busy before the printer caught up.

To avoid any corporate data security issues, and (frankly) to leave no fingerprints that would expose our use of expensive billable machine resources, we did this using a pure binary bootable card deck that used no IBM operating systems and did not access any disk files, only using the card reader and only writing to a printer.

I suspect it was the first and only time these 3 machines had ever run such a program with these obscure characteristics.

We sychronized the mainframe IPL's by phone, and shared progress by the same mechanism.

The result was predictable. Dave's million-dollar machine printed only one page before gasping for breath. My $7M machine lasted over a dozen pages.

Darryl's giant machine, of course, went on and on. Maybe it's still running..

Dave and I were amused and pleased by the experiment. Darryl was triumphant.

December, 1973
Dave Joins the US Army

When Dave was 20, he was working in high tech (specificaly, computers). Instead of pursuing that career, he heard the call to serve his country, and joined the US Army.

I recall being surprised that Dave would make such a dramatic change. Perhaps I shouldn't have been.

Some thirty years earlier, when our father was 20 years old, he was working in high tech (electronics). Then he heard the call to serve his country, and joined the US Army.

The US Army was a transformational life change for both. It was one of many things that led Dad and Dave to grow closer and closer as the years went on.

Dave completed Basic Combat Training (BCT) at Fort Leonard Wood, MO in March, 1974. Then he moved to Fort Knox, Kentucky, where he helped develop training materials, as an illustrator.

1976(?)

Easter Break Road Trip

I was at the University of Illinois, 120 miles from Naperville. Dave, at Fort Knox, was 400 miles away from Naperville, if I recall. We both visited home when we could, which was more often than one might think.

Then Easter Weekend rolled around, and we crafted a Holle Boys trip around the south-eastern part of the USA. Maybe 2000 miles. It was crowded and inconvenient... at times, it violated the laws of various states, but at no point did it violate the laws of physics. Deep South. Appalachians. On and on.

At one point we were in someplace like... Alabama. In some sort of place like... Waffle House, or Denny's. It was actually Easter Sunday. They had one of fhose plastic gizmo's on the table where they slide in deal-of-the-day advertisements.

Dave went to work, and created a custom advertisement that was very convincing, offering Rabbit Stew, with a convincing looking illustration of a forlorn Easter Bunny, surrounded by Easter Eggs, looking at you like he was begging for mercy... featuring a very attractive sales price.

Then we left the advertisement on the table.

I am thinking that, later that day, somebody probably ordered that stew. Dave's gift to them was a memorable (albeit embarassing) life experience that is still remembered, 50 years later.

1977

Dave Trasitions from Army to Artwork

Terre Haute, New Hampshire, Cape Cod, Triton Lane, 815 Douglas...

After Fort Knox, Dave rolled up his sleeves and cracked on with an effort to become a professional cartoonist. This is no small feat: ask Scott Adams, who (before becoming the most successful cartoonist of all time) suffered through a period where no sane person would continue.

Dave went through the same gauntlet, and for him, it just didn't work.

Did he break into the big leagues? Yes. He sold cartoons to major publications like the Cosmopolitan and the Atlantic. But these "big deals" were only worth something like $300 apiece for Dave... one-offs, after many months of sending cartoons and getting rejected.

A very tough time for Dave. And not a sustainable source of income.

2005: We Lost Our Dad

Dad was arguably the most important person in Dave's life. Dad supported Dave when he needed it most, and, perhaps, vice-versa.

For many years, they lived together... first, at our family home at 815 Douglas; after that, in an apartment, conveniently close to Portillo's, on Route 59 and West Jefferson.

They were inseparable... until Dad passed away. He was 84.

Dave was devastated. I don't think Dave ever really recovered.

2008(???)

Dave Goes To MSHV

When Dad passed away, Dave was devastated. Dad and Dave were deeply interdependent. Dave found himself in an emotional and financial downward spiral he could not handle on his own.

After a difficult period, Dave found refuge at the Midwest Shelter for Homeless Veterans (MSHV). His relationship with MSHV... first, as a resident, then later, as an employee... helped Dave when he needed it most, and provided him with a sense of purpose and a fulfilling role that restored his self-confidence, and allowed him to give back support to others for the rest of his life.

Late November, 2025

Ending Days

A month and a half later, his condition changed for the worse.

  • Thursday, 20 November: Dave did not show up for work, and did not call in. That combination was unusual. Robert London, a long-time friend and co-worker, went to check on Dave. He found Dave was not feeling well, but Dave reassured Robert he would be back in the office in the morning.
  • Friday, 21 November: Dave did not show up again. This time, when Robert checked on Dave, Dave was incoherent. Robert called an ambulance. Dave arrived at Central DuPage Hospital, in intensive care. Now Dave was responsive and coherent. There was good reason for optimism, especially since a scan showed no evidence of strokes or heart failure.
  • However, Dave became unresponsive, and went on a ventilator. A subsequent scan, later on Friday, showed he had suffered a massive stroke on one side of the brain. There were various signs that he might have been recovering and beginning to respond again, but for several days no conclusive indications of recovery presented themselves.
  • On xx November, Dave passed away peacefully.

What happened?

From what I've heard, the most likely final pathway was:
  • Dave's leukemia, and ongoing chemotherapy, led to
  • profound neutropenia, leading to
  • Pseudomonas UTI/bacteremia, leading to
  • overwhelming sepsis + septic emboli and/or mycotic aneurysm in the brain, leading to
  • massive ischemic and/or hemorrhagic stroke that developed after the first scan.
Pseudomonas is one of the organisms doctors fear most in this exact patient population for precisely this reason. Even with the very best antibiotics and ICU care, once it reaches that stage the outcome is often unsurvivable.

Goodbye, big brother...
